# Flowing Water

Rivers, streams, waterfalls and ponds for Godot 4.7. Draw a river down a
slope, press **Bake**, and get water that flows correctly around rocks,
reacts to the player, carries floating objects, and runs well on a Steam
Deck. Pure GDScript and shaders — no GDExtension, nothing to compile.

The flow is **baked once** from the river bed and the obstacles (the same
pipeline as the mountain-stream prototype: depth → Manning speed → constant
discharge → pressure projection on face fluxes → wake, foam, aeration,
surface offset). The game only draws it; gameplay queries read baked CPU
arrays, never the GPU.

---

## Installing and enabling

1. Copy `addons/flowing_water/` into your project.
2. Project → Project Settings → Plugins → enable **Flowing Water**.

Enabling registers the `WaterManager` autoload, the gizmos, the inspector
buttons and the shader globals (written to your project settings so exports
work). Disabling removes the autoload and editor hooks; the shader-global
declarations stay (they are inert and removing them would break saved water
scenes).

The plugin is self-contained and works in an empty project.

generated branch/blade cards.

development project; that tool is not needed to use them.)

## Drawing a river

1. Add a **WaterRiver** node. It is a `Path3D`: edit the curve with the
   normal path tools. The curve marks the **water surface** line.
2. Drag the handles on each side of every point to set the width there.
3. Use the inspector buttons:
   - **Snap to terrain** — drops every point onto the colliders below
     (+`snap_surface_height`).
   - **Make downhill** — lowers points so the path only descends. A scene
     warning appears while any part runs uphill.
   - **Rebuild mesh** — rebuilds the ribbon (rows every 0.5 m, 0.25 m when
     steeper than 15 %, 0.1 m on waterfalls; ~40 m chunks, one material).
   - **Bake** — raycasts the bed against `collision_mask`, runs the solver
     on a worker thread (progress on the button), and saves the result as
     `.res` next to the scene.

**Waterfalls** need no special node: wherever the curve drops steeper than
`waterfall_threshold` (0.3), the river becomes a fall. It is still the
same river - the bake accelerates the water with gravity
(`v = sqrt(v0^2 + 2 g h)`), so speed and white water rise smoothly through
the lip. What a fall adds, following how real falls and production game
falls are built:

- **A free-fall arc**: the surface leaves the lip in a parabola. The
  horizontal distance between the lip and the foot of your curve is how
  far the fastest water is thrown.
- **A brink per column, baked**: the fall does not start on one straight
  line across the ribbon. The bake compares the bed under every column
  with the channel just upstream: where the ground has dropped away the
  water is already in the air, up to 2.5 m above the curve's lip, so the
  sheet starts where the rock ends. Each column then falls on its own arc
  from its own brink, thrown as far as its baked speed carries it (slow
  shallow water at the sides lands closer to the rock, 75-100 % of the
  curve's throw), and a column that lands early becomes pool. A bank that
  is dry at the brink stays dry all the way down, so the sheet is only as
  wide as the water that reaches the edge. None of this costs anything at
  runtime: it is stored in the WaterData (`fall_lead`, `fall_throw`,
  `fall_sheet`, `fall_wet`, per-cell `cell_slope`) and built into the
  mesh. Re-bake after moving the terrain or rocks at a fall.
- **Thickness**: the fall is a sheet with an upper and a lower surface.
  It is as thick as the flow at the brink (0.715 of the flow depth, the
  classic free-overfall result) and thins as it accelerates, because
  thickness x speed stays constant. Each column starts from its own depth
  at the brink and the sheet thins out toward its sides, where the upper
  and lower surface meet. The surface also draws down over the last metre
  before the brink and leaves it at that height, and the river's own
  colour and foam carry on over the edge, so there is no seam between
  river and fall.
- **Aeration**: clear and glassy where it leaves the lip unbroken, white
  once it has taken in air, tearing into separate strands lower down.
  Thin water breaks up first: the baked thickness x speed (the water
  passing per metre of width) runs out toward the sides of the fall, and
  there the sheet frays into strands and beads instead of ending in a cut.
- **Structure**: a fall texture (curtains of uneven width, ridged strands,
  fibres, surges) scrolls on both surfaces at slightly different speeds -
  parallax through the sheet - at the water's own accelerating pace. Thin
  water between strands stays translucent. The sheet billows more the
  further it has fallen.
- **Impact**: drops fall alongside with the baked velocity, drawn as
  soft streaks along their own velocity that lengthen as they speed up
  (`render/water_droplet.gdshader`), in varied sizes and opacities, most
  of them shed by the thin edges of the sheet; the foot gets plunge foam,
  spray, mist, and rings that keep the pool moving.

**Banks**: the river needs ground to end against. Where water reaches the
side of the ribbon (the bank there is lower than the surface), the bake
draws the surface down to the bed over the last 0.9 m, and at low view
angles the shader trusts the baked depth rather than the depth buffer, so
the ribbon never hangs over a low bank as a flat slab. It is still better
to make the ribbon a little wider than the channel.

**Rocks in the water**: the surface never stops short of a rock. Cells
under an obstacle are filled from the water around them, so flow, foam and
waves run right up to it and the rock simply stands in the water, with a
wake behind it. A rock in a waterfall's path parts the sheet: water falls
on both sides, with a gap below the rock and a splash on top.

Rivers longer than 1 km are split into segments with their own textures,
overlapping one row so there is no seam.

## Drawing a pond

Add a **WaterPond**, move it to the water level (its Y **is** the level),
and drag the outline handles. Bake raycasts a world-space grid (0.25 m
cells, capped at 512², coarser for big ponds). A pond with no junctions has
zero flow and gets gentle wind ripples instead (strength from `WindField`
if your project has one).

## Junctions

A **WaterJunction** links one end of a river to a pond
(`RIVER_INTO_POND` or `POND_INTO_RIVER`) and enforces:

- **Height** — *Snap to pond level* flattens the river's last metres onto
  the pond level without a kink; a warning shows if the river arrives low.
- **Flow** — the river's last baked row becomes the pond's inflow boundary
  (a pale fan of foam and aeration spreads from the mouth); an outgoing
  river starts at the pond's speed. **Bake river + pond** bakes everything
  in the right order (inlet rivers → pond → outlet rivers). Never bake the
  pond before its rivers.
- **Look** — the river ribbon extends `blend_length` metres into the pond
  and fades out, drawn above the pond (render priority + 2 mm lift, so the
  two surfaces never flicker), while standing waves, foam, stretch and all
  style parameters blend from river to pond values. Both bodies must use
  the same shader variant (realistic/stylized); the editor warns if not.

A waterfall into a pond is just a river whose last rows are a fall: plunge
foam, mist and ripples appear at the landing point.

## Baking, and when to re-bake

Bake in the editor, ship the `.res`. The plugin **never re-bakes at
runtime**. If a
collider inside a body's bounds moves, the node shows **"Bake is out of
date"** in the scene tree — press Bake again; flow, foam and spray all
update. Target: a 300 m river bakes in a few seconds; the pressure solve
runs on a compute shader when a RenderingDevice exists, with a GDScript
fallback.

## Styles

A **WaterStyle** resource holds everything about the look; swap it without
re-baking. Make your own: right-click -> New Resource -> WaterStyle, or
duplicate a preset from `styles/`:

|---|---|
| `spring` | Crystal clear: every stone visible, strong caustics |
| `clear_mountain` | Clear mountain stream, a trace of rock flour, bright white water |
| `glacial` | Milky turquoise meltwater |
| `lowland` | Slow lowland river: green-brown, about half a metre of visibility, glassy slicks |
| `peat` | Forest/bog water: clear but stained dark amber by tannins |
| `muddy` | After heavy rain: opaque ochre, dirty foam |
| `stylized` | Saturated blue-green, soft colour bands, hard foam edges |
| `toon` | Three flat colour bands, thick white foam lines, sharp highlights |

`examples/05_styles.tscn` shows all eight on the same stream;
`examples/07_lowland_river.tscn` is a wide, slow river you can flip between
`lowland`, `peat` and `muddy`.

### From clear to muddy

Two different things make water hard to see through, and the style has a
control for each:

- **Absorption + Clarity** tint the water without clouding it. High
  absorption with low turbidity is tea-coloured bog water (`peat`): dark,
  but you still see the bed in the shallows.
- **Turbidity + Sediment colour** are suspended silt. Sediment scatters
  light back, so turbid water looks like a lit, coloured surface and the
  bed disappears within a visibility distance: turbidity 0.2 is roughly
  2 m, 0.4 about half a metre, 0.9 a few centimetres. `Turbidity
  variation` breaks it into clouds drifting with the current.
- **Surface slicks** add the smooth, glassy patches that slide along a
  slow, deep river and break up its ripples.

**Rain and floods at runtime**: `WaterManager.storm_turbidity` (0-1) is
added to every style, and `WaterManager.storm_color` is the mud it washes
in. Tween it up after rain and every river in the level turns brown
together - no re-bake, no material swaps. (Key **R** in the examples.)

### Caustics

Caustics are drawn **by the water surface itself**: it reconstructs the
point under each pixel from the depth buffer and brightens it with a
drifting light network. So they land on whatever is under the water -
your terrain, rocks, props, a character's legs - with **no changes to
those materials**. Per style: `Caustics strength` (0 = off) and `Caustics
size`. They focus a hand's depth down, fade in deep water, and are hidden
automatically by sediment, so a muddy river has none without you doing
anything.

High/Medium use two samples (a sharp network), Deck one. The Low preset
has no depth buffer, so there the alternative is material-side:
`water_caustics()` in `render/water_receiver.gdshaderinc` (the shipped
rock material has a `material_caustics` switch for it).

Two compiled shader variants (pick per style): **Realistic** (physically
based, refraction, depth-buffer thickness) and **Stylized** (banded depth
colour, hard foam edges, shoreline foam lines, optional depth-gradient
texture, sharp highlights - and the cheapest to render).

## Character and floating-object API

**WaterInteractor** (child of your character). The plugin never moves your
character; it reports and your controller decides:

```gdscript
var in_water: bool
var depth_at_feet: float
var submerged_ratio: float          # 0-1 of `height`
func get_push() -> Vector3          # force from the current, ∝ depth × speed²
signal entered_water(speed)
signal exited_water()
signal swept_away()                 # push exceeded `sweep_threshold`
```

It injects ripples while moving through water and a ring + splash on entry.

**WaterFloater** (child of a RigidBody3D): 4–8 sample points
(auto-generated from the collision shape, or your own `Marker3D` children),
per-point buoyancy so the body tilts with slope and waves, drag toward
`get_flow()` so it drifts downstream and turns in eddies. Forces are
clamped per step; sleeps out of water; falls freely over waterfalls.

**Queries** (`WaterManager` autoload — baked CPU arrays, no GPU readback):

```gdscript
WaterManager.get_height(pos)   # NAN if no water here
WaterManager.get_flow(pos)     # Vector3, m/s
WaterManager.get_depth(pos)
WaterManager.get_body(pos)
WaterManager.add_ripple(pos, strength, radius)
WaterManager.storm_turbidity = 0.6   # rain: every river turns muddy
WaterManager.storm_color = Color(0.33, 0.24, 0.13)
```

In a junction blend zone the queries return the weighted mix of both bodies.

**Receiving the water in your materials**: include
`render/water_receiver.gdshaderinc` in your terrain/rock shaders for
caustics below the surface and a dark glossy wet band up to 0.3 m above it
(see `examples/shared/terrain.gdshader` in the repository). A ready-made rock material is
`render/rock_receiver.gdshader`. For materials you cannot edit, enable
`bank_wet_decals` on the river for a decal strip along both banks.

## Underwater

When the camera goes below the surface of a water body, the view takes on
that body's style from the inside. Nothing to set up: `WaterManager` adds
a compositor effect (`render/underwater_effect.gd` + `underwater.glsl`) to
the active camera and feeds it every frame. It works on any material,
because everything is reconstructed from the depth buffer.

- **Fog with real absorption.** The same Beer-Lambert numbers as the
  surface: the style's `Absorption`/`Clarity` per colour channel plus the
  sediment from `Turbidity`. Red goes first, so distance turns blue-green
  in clear water and brown in silt. `spring` sees across a pool; `lowland`
  about half a metre; `muddy` a hand's length. The scattered light is
  brighter looking up and darker looking down, and all of it darkens as
  the camera goes deeper.
- **Caustics on everything below the surface**, projected along the
  refracted sun direction, drifting with the current, fading with depth
  and silt (style: `Caustics strength`, `Caustics size`). Nothing above
  the waterline is lit; the caustics the surface draws when seen from
  above are not drawn twice.
- **Light shafts** (Medium/High): the same light network marched through
  the water, so shafts end on the caustics they cast. A trace of silt
  makes them visible, a lot swallows them.
- **Suspended particles** (Medium/High) that stay put in the water, drift
  with the flow and multiply with turbidity.
- **Lens** (Medium/High): a slight wavy distortion, a mild blur with
  distance, a soft vignette.
- **Waterline.** With the camera half in the water the split follows the
  water height across the screen - including the player's ripples - with
  a thin meniscus line, instead of switching the whole screen.
- **The surface from below**: a bright Snell's window overhead; outside it
  the surface is a mirror (total internal reflection) that on Medium/High
  reflects the bed and rocks beneath it.

Settings:

| Where | Property | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| WaterStyle > Underwater | `underwater_fog_density` | Scales absorption and sediment for the view from below (1 = same water as from above) |
| WaterStyle > Underwater | `underwater_shafts` | Strength of the sun shafts, 0 = none |
| WaterStyle > Underwater | `underwater_particles` | Amount of drifting particles, 0 = none |
| WaterStyle > Caustics | `caustics_strength`, `caustics_size` | Shared with the surface |
| WaterManager | `underwater_enabled` | Master switch |
| WaterManager | `underwater_caustics` | Multiplies every style's caustics below the surface |
| WaterManager | `underwater_distortion` | Lens wobble and blur, 0 = off |
| WaterManager | `underwater_vignette` | Corner darkening, 0 = off |
| WaterManager | `sun_light` | The DirectionalLight3D for caustics and shafts (default: the first visible one) |

`WaterManager.is_camera_underwater()` tells gameplay code when the view
is active. `storm_turbidity` muddies the underwater view together with
the surface.

Cost of the pass at 1280x800 on the development machine (Apple M4 Pro,
frame-time difference with the effect on and off, `tools/uw_bench.tscn`):
High about 0.55 ms, Medium 0.4 ms, Deck/Low 0.15 ms. Not yet measured on
a Steam Deck.

Limits: the waterline uses the queried water height plus the ripple
simulation, not the standing waves of rapids, so in rough water it can
sit a few centimetres off the drawn surface. Shafts and caustics are not
shadowed by rocks or banks. If your own materials call `water_caustics()`
(the Low-preset path), they add to these under water - switch one of the
two off. The pass needs Forward+ (a half-float colour buffer); on
Compatibility the view falls back to plain environment fog.

## Shadows on white water

White water shows shadow noise more than anything else in a scene. With a
soft sun (`light_angular_distance` above 0) and Godot's default shadow
filter, a waterfall in the dappled shade of trees gets a fine speckle that
makes it look like cloth. Raise *Project Settings → Rendering → Lights and
Shadows → Directional Shadow → Soft Shadow Filter Quality* to **Soft High**
(this project does), or use a hard sun. Water and spray do not cast shadows
themselves.

## Quality presets and Steam Deck

`WaterManager.quality` ∈ `DECK, LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH`. On a Steam Deck
(detected via the `SteamDeck=1` environment variable Steam sets) the Deck
preset is picked automatically at startup; set `quality` yourself to
override.

| Setting | Deck | Low | Medium | High |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Detail layers | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| Refraction | simple | off | full | full |
| Vertex waves beyond | 25 m off | 20 m off | 40 m off | 60 m off |
| Ripple grid | 128² | off | 192² | 256² |
| Ripple steps/frame | 1 | – | 2 | 2 |
| World water map | 128² | 128² | 256² | 256² |
| Caustics (from the surface) | 1 sample | off | 2 samples | 2 samples |
| Spray amount | 35 % | 20 % | 70 % | 100 % |
| Underwater | fog, 1-sample caustics, waterline | same as Deck | + shafts (6 steps), 2 particle shells, lens, bed mirror | + shafts (12 steps), 4 particle shells |

Quality levels are **compiled shader variants** (`render/water_*.gdshader`
are thin `#define` wrappers around one shader body), not runtime branches.
Texture reads per pixel on the water surface (when the ripple grid is
active near the player; 3 fewer when not):

| Variant | Reads/px (fragment) |
|---|---|
| `water_full` (High/Medium) | 19 base (2 baked + 1 jitter + 6 detail + 3 ripple + 2 screen + 2 depth + 2 caustics, +1 mip-blurred), +3 for the fine foam layer within 14 m of the camera where there is foam, plus screen-space reflections on smooth water: up to 18 depth taps + 1 colour, early-out on first hit and skipped under foam/falls |
| `water_deck` | 13 (2 + 1 + 3 detail + 3 ripple + 2 screen + 1 depth + 1 caustics), no SSR, no fine foam |
| `water_low` | 9 (no screen/depth textures at all) |
| `water_far` (distance LOD) | 6 |

Screen-space reflections (banks, rocks and trees mirrored in calm water)
are compiled only into `water_full`; the Deck preset keeps sky reflections
only.

Far chunks switch to the `_far` variant with hysteresis at a distance where
vertex waves and refraction have already faded to zero, so there is no pop.
Water never stacks: only the junction blend zone is drawn twice. Bodies
outside camera range skip particles, audio and low-rate their floaters.

**Renderers**: Forward+ and Mobile are fully supported. Compatibility runs
with these fallbacks: player ripples **off** (one warning — it has no
compute shaders; see "Changes from the plan"), bake pressure solve on the
CPU, underwater as environment fog, thickness from the baked depth.

**Benchmark**: run `examples/demo/benchmark.tscn` (ideally at 1280×800 on the Deck).
It flies a fixed path through the demo at all four presets, twice each
(water shown/hidden), and reports average/worst frame time, GPU time and
the water-only GPU cost to the console and `user://water_benchmark.txt`.
Budgets: Deck ≤ 2.5 ms at 1280×800, Medium ≤ 2.5 ms at 1080p mid-range.

Measured on the development machine (Apple M4 Pro, 1280x800, Forward+,

Low:    frame avg 3.08 ms, worst 18.5 ms, water frame delta ~0.46 ms

run `benchmark.tscn` on yours (Vulkan reports real GPU timings) and send

## Connecting to wind, fog and the larger water system

At startup the manager looks for `WindField`, `FogSim` and `WaterSystem`
autoloads (one adapter: `_resolve_adapters()` in `water_manager.gd`). If
present, mist drifts with `WindField.get_wind()` and pond wind-ripples
scale with it. If absent, nothing breaks and nothing is logged.

## Changes from the plan, and why

1. **World water map is built on the CPU, not with a SubViewport render.**
   The plan called for an orthographic SubViewport with a data material.
   A 3D viewport's output buffer is 8-bit sRGB after tonemapping (HDR float
   output only exists for the 2D path), which is not enough precision for
   surface heights, and behaves differently per renderer. Instead each
   water body splats its baked grid straight into an RGBAH buffer
   (`splat_water_map`), a bounded number of texels per frame, and the
   texture swaps when complete. Exact float data, identical on every
   renderer, no render-layer bookkeeping, and about a millisecond of CPU
   on the frames that rebuild. The map also carries the water level on
   the banks beside the water, so wet bands filter cleanly at the
   shoreline. `render/world_map.gdshader` from the planned tree therefore
   does not exist.
2. **Compatibility renderer: ripples are off** (with one warning) rather
   than re-implemented as a SubViewport ping-pong. Maintaining a second
   full simulation backend doubles the shader surface for the renderer
   that can least afford the cost; everything else (flow, foam, floaters,
   queries, wet banks) works there.
3. **Pressure solve** optionally runs as a red-black SOR compute shader on
   a local RenderingDevice (`bake/bake_pressure.glsl`) when grids are
   large, since plain GDScript misses the 10 s target on long rivers; the
   GDScript loop remains as reference and fallback.
4. **Mesh frame attributes**: instead of the prototype's `aFrame` custom
   attribute, the ribbon stores the downstream direction in `TANGENT` and
   the base normal in `NORMAL` (the curve bends in Godot, so the frame must
   vary per vertex anyway); `COLOR` carries the junction fade/lift masks.
   `CUSTOM0` is unused.
5. **Far-distance LOD** does not cross-fade two overlapping surfaces
   (pitfall 8): instead, the expensive effects (vertex waves, refraction)
   fade with distance *inside* the full shader, and the chunk switches to
   the cheap variant with hysteresis only past the distance where they
   have already faded out, so the switch is invisible.
6. **Underwater effect** uses a uniform buffer instead of push constants
   (the parameter block exceeds the 128-byte push-constant floor — the
   Steam Deck's limit). It runs on every preset (Deck/Low leave out the
   shafts, particles and lens); only the Compatibility renderer falls back
   to environment fog.
7. **`EditorInterface`** is accessed through `Engine.get_singleton()` in
   runtime-reachable scripts, because the class is absent from export
   templates.
8. **Spray** uses one custom particles shader fed by an
   importance-expanded emission-point table (the prototype's cumulative-sum
   sampling, done once on the CPU), rather than per-kind hand placement.

9. **Pond inflow has momentum.** A pure pressure solve spreads a river's
   discharge so thinly that a pond is almost still (centimetres per
   second). The pond bake now seeds a decaying jet from each inlet along
   the river's direction *before* the projection, so the same
   mass-conserving solve yields the fan at the mouth, a visible current
   and return eddies. The inflow is handed to the pond where the river
   crosses the shore (not at the ribbon's tip), so there is no dead zone
   in the blend.
10. **Floaters work free.** Like the prototype's logs ("ran aground: slide
   toward the middle"), a `WaterFloater` that jams on a rock or beaches
   with moving water under it is nudged toward deeper water. Buoyancy is
   draft-based and drag is relative to the water velocity (absolute
   damping made bodies drift at half the current's speed).
11. **Edges and junction fades use real alpha**, not a mix toward the
   screen texture: Godot's screen texture holds only the opaque pass, so
   a river fading over a pond would otherwise fade to the bare bed.
12. **Foam pattern** is a warped cell-wall lace in the shared noise
   texture's alpha (thin connected lines first, closed white water last)
   instead of the prototype's ridged noise, and foam coverage follows a
   curve so calm reaches stay clear.
13. **Higher falls churn a longer plunge reach** (2.7 m scaled by the
   square root of the drop, up to 2.2x).

14. **Sediment is a separate model from absorption** (turbidity, sediment
   colour, drifting silt clouds, runtime storm turbidity), replacing the
   single "milky" control: muddy water is lit scattering, not just a
   darker tint.
15. **Caustics moved into the water shader** (from the depth buffer), so
   they need no receiver materials; the receiver include keeps the wet
   band and an optional material-side caustics function for Low.
16. **Ripples travel at ripple speed** (about a metre per second, slower
   in shallows) rather than the long-wave speed, and the surface shader
   takes their slope per texel.

17. **Waterfalls are accelerated rivers, not a separate effect.** The
   first version switched to a flat white sheet above the slope
   threshold. Now the bake carries speed and foam smoothly through the
   lip; the ribbon follows a free-fall parabola between the lip and the
   foot; a dedicated fall texture and falling droplets (born with the
   baked velocity) add the structure of falling water.
18. **Cells under rocks are filled in** from their neighbours after the
   solve (`WaterData.obstacle` keeps the true mask), so there is no calm,
   transparent ring around obstacles. Ripples still bounce off them.
19. **Example scenes use CC0 Poly Haven textures and rock scans**, and
   are centred on the origin.

20. **Waterfalls have thickness and aeration.** Research into free
   overfalls and game waterfalls (layered meshes, scrolling fluid
   textures, erosion, impact particles) led to a two-surface sheet whose
   thickness follows the flow, clear at the lip and white lower down.
